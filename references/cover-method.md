# Cover method

## Strategy

- Separate the article's broad topic from the single conflict, discovery, or conclusion that can be understood visually.
- Favor one visual sentence over a feature inventory. The cover should provoke one relevant question within three seconds.
- Keep titles truthful, concrete, and normally 4–9 English words. Recheck the title after generation; it must describe the actual image.
- Make the three directions differ by narrative mechanism: scene, minimal concept, or editorial metaphor. Adapt these categories rather than applying them mechanically.

For each direction specify: concept, scene, primary and secondary elements, composition, headline location, connection to the thesis, and thumbnail risk.

## Prompt skeleton

Write each prompt as compact prose or labeled lines covering:

1. LinkedIn article cover; horizontal 1.91:1.
2. Primary concept and what relationship the image must communicate.
3. Subject position and approximate frame share.
4. Secondary elements and visual hierarchy.
5. Headline-safe area: normally 35–40% clean, low-detail space.
6. Lighting and restrained palette tied to meaning.
7. Large simple shapes that survive a phone thumbnail.
8. No image-generated text.
9. A short, topic-specific avoid list.

Do not stack synonyms. Do not repeat the same constraint in multiple forms unless image editing has already violated it.

## Mobile-first QA

Assess the original and an approximately 360px-wide preview.

| Check | Pass condition |
|---|---|
| Thesis | The main relationship matches the article and does not exaggerate it. |
| Three-second read | One dominant idea is understandable without inspecting details. |
| Subject | Primary subject remains recognizable in the thumbnail. |
| Hierarchy | Primary, secondary, and contextual elements remain ordered. |
| Headline space | At least roughly 35% is quiet, high-contrast, and uninterrupted. |
| Ambiguity | Objects do not imply the wrong product, action, direction, or setting. |
| Cleanliness | No generated words, logos, tiny UI, clutter, or unrelated decoration. |
| Title fit | Recommended title describes what the generated image actually shows. |

Fail and revise when thesis, headline space, text/logo cleanliness, or thumbnail recognition fails. Treat other issues proportionally.

## Revision method

- Name the single observed defect before editing.
- Preserve passed composition, aspect ratio, palette, lighting, negative space, and subjects as explicit invariants.
- Describe geometry literally: which side, travel direction, stop point, relative size, and what must be absent.
- Inspect the result. Image models may ignore direction, barriers, counts, or negative constraints.
- Stop when the image is publishable; do not chase cosmetic gains that risk semantic drift.

## Proven lessons from the Tavus workflow

- Visualize the capability-versus-context conflict, not the full product feature list.
- A lens or perception terminal can read as surveillance, a projector, a smart speaker, or access control; check the actual silhouette and action.
- Signal direction must match the intended information flow. If a boundary blocks it, verify no faint line continues beyond the boundary.
- A regulatory boundary should not accidentally look like an ordinary wall, doorway, laser, or warning tape unless that reading is intended.
- “No faces” includes facial features, profiles, and recognizable facial contours; use a blank geometric human silhouette when people are needed.
- Environmental cues must be few, large, and semantically useful at thumbnail size.
- A title using a visual noun such as “face” is unsuitable when that noun is absent from the final image.
